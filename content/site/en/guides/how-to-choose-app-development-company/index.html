Before you look for a company: know what you are buying

Most disputes between a client and an app company start with the word "app" itself. One side may mean a shell that shows the website; the other may mean two native apps with a backend and an admin panel. Before you ask for any proposal, write one page: who uses the app, the one thing they must get done in it, and what happens behind the scenes after they tap the button.

That page is enough to tell you what kind of company you need. An app that shows content and an order form can be built by a small team. An app with payments, tracking, drivers and settlements needs a company with designers, backend developers and testers, not a front-end developer alone. And if you cannot write the page, start with a short requirements study before comparing prices, because proposals written against a vague description cannot be compared.

App-specific questions to ask before you sign

Some questions suit any software company. These only show their weight with apps. Ask every company in the same words and write the answers down.

  • Which published apps have you built that I can download now? Try them on your phone; do not settle for pictures in a deck.
  • What will you build my app with, and why? A good answer ties the technology to what your app needs, not only to what the team knows.
  • In whose name will the App Store and Google Play accounts be opened? The only acceptable answer: your company’s.
  • Who builds the backend and admin panel, and where are they hosted? Do I get access from day one?
  • How do I see progress? Ask for a test build installed on your phone every week or two.
  • Which devices do you test on? Ask about mid-range Android phones, small screens and weak connections.
  • What do you do if the store rejects the app at review? An experienced company has been through it and knows the causes.
  • Who updates the app when a new iOS or Android version is released, and is that inside the support contract?
  • How do you handle user data? Ask about encryption, permissions and where data is stored, and check with your adviser what the Personal Data Protection Law requires of you.
  • What do I receive at the end? Source code, signing keys, the accounts for third-party services, and a document explaining how the app is built and released.
  • Could another team take over the code and continue? A confident yes is a sign of organised code.

Red flags in app proposals

These signs recur in projects that go wrong, and all of them are visible before signing to anyone who looks.

  • A one-page offer with a number and a duration and no list of screens.
  • A price for an app on both platforms with backend, admin panel and payments that sits far below the market range. Ask what was left out.
  • The offer does not mention the backend, the admin panel or publishing to the stores. Whatever is not written will be requested later at a new price.
  • The company suggests publishing on its own account "to make things easier". Moving a live app between accounts is a long procedure, and it can become leverage.
  • Past work shown as pictures only, or store apps that have not been updated in a long time.
  • A large advance not tied to any delivery, or a big discount for paying in full.
  • A very short timeline promised for a multi-sided app with no explanation of how.
  • Every question answered "yes, possible" without one question about your business. Someone who does not ask does not understand what they are about to build.
  • Nobody talks about testing or about what happens after launch.

How to check a company’s work in an hour

You need no technical background to vet an app company. The two stores show what you need for free. Open the page of any app the company says it built and look at the developer name: is it the client or the vendor? The first means the company hands accounts over to its clients. Then look at the date of the last update, and at user reviews and what they complain about.

Next, download the app and use it for five minutes the way a customer would: sign up, browse, switch the connection off and on, change the language if there is one. An app that stumbles in those minutes tells you about the level of testing. Finally, ask to speak to a past client and ask only two things: was the project delivered on its date, and what happened when the first problem appeared after launch?

Why price alone is a poor yardstick

With us a simple app or first version starts from EGP 180,000 and usually costs EGP 180,000 to 450,000, and a mid-sized app on both platforms with an admin panel and payments EGP 450,000 to 1,050,000. Offers in the market vary widely because they do not describe the same product. Two proposals with the same title can differ in the number of screens, whether there is an admin panel, who pays third-party service fees and whether testing is included.

So put the offers in one table with the same line items before you look at the final number. Often the dearer offer is the cheaper one in practice, because it includes what the other will later charge as changes. And sometimes the cheaper offer is the right one: if what you really need is a simple app for an untested idea, there is no reason to pay for an advanced platform.

Add the following years to the comparison, not the build alone. Maintenance in the market runs at 15% to 20% of the build cost a year, and monthly support plans at EGP 22,500 to 180,000. A company that builds cheaply and then disappears costs you a full rebuild.

After launch: half of the decision

An app is a living product. Apple and Google release new versions of their systems every year and update their store policies, and an app that is not updated starts to break on new devices or has its updates rejected. So before you sign, ask about the company a year from now, not the company on delivery day.

Ask for the proposal to state what support covers after launch, within how many hours a fault report is answered, who watches crashes and their reports, and what a maintenance contract costs once the covered period ends. And make sure an up-to-date copy of the code sits in a repository you own, so your product does not depend on one relationship continuing.

When do you not need an app company at all?

If your customer deals with you once or twice a year, or what you need is to present services and take enquiries, a fast website that works well on a phone is enough and costs clearly less. If you sell ordinary products, a store on a ready-made platform with its ready-made app may spare you custom code. An app earns its cost when the user returns often, or you need notifications, or device features such as the camera and location.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know an app development company is trustworthy?

Check three things you can see for yourself: published apps you can download and try, a written proposal with a list of screens, stages and payments tied to delivery, and a willingness to register the code and both store accounts in your company’s name. Then talk to a past client about deadlines and about how things went after launch. Verbal promises do not count.

The price depends on the number of screens, user roles and integrations, and you get a written quote within 24 hours.

What is the single most important question to ask an app company?

In whose name will the store accounts, the code and the server be? If the answer is your company’s, from day one, you own your product and can change vendor whenever you want. If the answer is anything else, every promise that follows is worth less, because your app’s future then depends on one party.

Should I pick a company inside Saudi Arabia or work remotely?

Location matters less than the way of working. What matters is that the company works during your hours, understands the Saudi market and its requirements such as local payment gateways and e-invoicing, and that you have a written contract, regular meetings and periodic test builds. Ask each company how it will run the project week by week, and compare the answers.

Does the app have to be on iOS and Android from the start?

Both platforms are widespread in Egypt, so most companies build the two together with a shared framework that writes the code once. On a very tight budget you can start with one platform, but choose it by your audience. And ask the company whether the code will serve the second platform later without a rewrite.

How large an advance payment is reasonable?

There is no single correct share. The rule is that every payment is tied to something you receive: one at signing to start the work, then payments at design approval and with each test build, and a final one at release. Be wary of any offer that asks for most of the money before you have seen work, or that gives a large discount for paying in full.

What if the App Store or Google Play rejects the app?

Rejection at review is common and has well-known causes, such as a gap in the privacy policy, missing test-account details or the way in-app payment is handled. An experienced company deals with the notes and resubmits. Ask for publishing and following the review through to approval to be part of the proposal, and do not tie your marketing campaign to delivery day itself.

Do I need a requirements study before asking for proposals?

If your idea is large or loosely defined, yes. A short study produces a list of screens and functions and a time estimate, and you can send it to several companies and get offers on one basis. If the app is small and clear, a page describing the user, the goal and the main journey is enough.
