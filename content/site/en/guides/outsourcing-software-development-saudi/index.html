Outsourcing software development from Saudi Arabia: models, managing a remote team, and the risks

Get a quote on WhatsAppRequest a quote

Outsourcing works when you pick the model that matches how clear your project is: a fixed scope at a fixed price if you know what you want in detail, a dedicated team on a monthly contract if the product will evolve, and staff augmentation if you have a technical lead who is short of people. Most of what buyers fear from outsourcing, a vendor who vanishes, lost code and late delivery, is controlled by four things: code and accounts in your name from day one, payments tied to working deliveries, a test build every two weeks, and one person on your side who owns the decisions. This guide is for business owners and technology managers in Egypt considering having a website, an app or a system built by an outside party.

What is outsourcing, and when does it make sense?

Outsourcing means handing the building of your software product, or part of it, to a party outside your company instead of hiring an in-house team. That party may be in your city or in another country, and the principle is the same: you buy ready execution capacity instead of building it.

It makes sense when software is not your core business. A contracting, distribution or clinic company needs a system that works, not a software engineering department. Hiring a full team, meaning a designer, a front-end developer, a backend developer and a tester, for a project lasting months is a long commitment for a temporary need. And one developer’s salary sits in the market at EGP 120,000 to 300,000 a month, for one person, not a team, with no cover when they are away.

An in-house team is the better answer when the software itself is the product you sell and develop every day, and you have someone to lead the work technically. Many companies combine the two: an outside party builds, and an internal person owns the product and sets priorities.

The three models and how to choose between them

Fixed scope is the simplest for the buyer: you agree a list of what will be built, its price and its date, and pay in stages. It suits projects with clear edges. Its weakness is that any change needs a negotiation, and that the vendor prices in a margin for the unknown. So it succeeds to the extent the scope is precise, and turns into a dispute to the extent it is vague.

A dedicated team is a monthly contract for a team working on your product only, or for a set share of its time. There is no final list, only priorities you set in short cycles, and you see the result of each cycle. It suits products that learn from their users and change. Its weakness is that the cost is open-ended unless someone on your side sets priorities and says "that is enough".

Staff augmentation means taking on one or more developers who join your team and work with your tools under your management. It suits a company with a technical lead and an established way of working that is short of people or of one skill. It does not suit a company with no technical management, because you are buying hands, not a result, and responsibility for the output is yours.

The rule for choosing: the clearer the brief, the more the balance tips towards fixed scope; the longer-lived and more changeable the product, the more it tips towards a dedicated team. Many start with a first version on a fixed scope, then move to a monthly contract for ongoing development after launch.

How do you manage a team that works remotely?

Remote management does not need complex tools. It needs a steady rhythm everyone knows. These practices are enough for most projects:

  • One person on your side who owns the decisions, and one on the vendor’s side who runs the project. No scattered messages to developers.
  • A short weekly meeting at a fixed time: what was done, what is next, what is waiting on a decision from you.
  • A working test build every week or two, on a link or on your phone. It is the only dependable measure of progress.
  • One daily channel for quick questions, such as a WhatsApp group or Slack, and a written record of decisions.
  • A task board you can see: what is to do, what is in progress, what is done.
  • Overlapping working hours written into the contract, within your working days and time zone.
  • An agreed response time for both sides, so work does not stall for days waiting for an answer.
  • Every decision that changes the scope is written down, even in one line, before it is carried out.

Ownership and access: what protects you more than any other clause

Most bad outsourcing stories end the same way: the project stopped, and the client owns nothing to continue with. Preventing that is simple, and it is done in the first week, not the last.

Open the code repository, the server, the domain, the store accounts and the payment gateway yourself, in your company’s name, and give the vendor access to work on them. Ask for the code to be pushed to your repository daily or weekly, not at final delivery. And ask for a short document explaining how the project is run and released. Then if the relationship ends for any reason, another team picks up where the first stopped.

Write into the contract that ownership of the code written for your project passes to you, and when. Drafting that clause, and the termination and dispute clauses, especially if the vendor is in another country, is a legal matter for your lawyer.

Quality: how do you judge it if you are not technical?

You do not need to read code to assess the work. There are signs visible from outside. Does the test build work as described, or break at the first unexpected use? Were all the notes you sent closed, or did some come back? Does the team tell you about problems before you find them? A team that only ever brings good news is hiding something.

Ask about specific practices too. Does a second developer review every change before it is merged? Are there automated tests for sensitive parts such as calculations and payments? Is there a staging environment separate from the one your customers use? Are backups taken, and has restoring them been tried?

For large projects, bring in an independent technical reviewer for a few hours mid-project and before acceptance. The cost is small against what it reveals, and having one from the start raises the standard of the work.

Data, confidentiality and local requirements

When you hand work to an outside party, your responsibility for your customers’ and staff’s data remains. Saudi Arabia’s Personal Data Protection Law places duties on whoever collects data and on whoever processes it on their behalf, and it contains provisions on transferring data outside the Kingdom. Exactly what is required of you is for your adviser or the regulator to confirm. We give no legal advice here.

In practice you can lower the risk with simple decisions: the team works on test data, not real data; access to the live environment is given to as few people as possible and withdrawn when no longer needed; and the contract states where hosting is, the confidentiality duties and what happens to the data when the engagement ends. Sign a non-disclosure agreement before sharing any sensitive information.

And ask the vendor what it knows about the specifics of the Saudi market: e-invoicing, local payment methods such as mada, Arabic and right-to-left interfaces, and Hijri dates. A team that has never built for this market will learn at your project’s expense.

How to start with the least risk

Do not hand a large project to a party you have not tried. Start with a small paid piece that has a clear result: a requirements study, the interface design, or one module. Within a few weeks you will know more about how the team works than ten meetings would tell you: whether it keeps dates, asks the right questions and replies on time.

Before that, ask for work that is live now and that you can try, and talk to a past client. Ask who will work on your project, by name, not who the company’s members are. And compare proposals by line item, not by the final number: what is included, what the stages are, and who owns what.

Frequently asked questions

What does outsourcing software development mean?

It means handing the building of your website, app or system to a party outside your company instead of hiring an in-house team. There are three common models: a project with a fixed scope and price, a dedicated team on a monthly contract, and staff augmentation with developers working under your management. It suits companies that need the software and do not want to run software engineering.

Which is better, a fixed scope or a dedicated team?

A fixed scope is better for a project with clear edges whose details you know in advance, because the price and date are set. A dedicated team is better for a product that will change and evolve, because you reorder priorities without a negotiation each time. Many start with a first version on a fixed scope and move to a monthly contract after launch.

Is outsourcing cheaper than hiring developers?

It depends on how long you need them. For a project lasting months, outsourcing is usually cheaper, because you get a full team with no ongoing salaries. One in-house developer’s salary sits in the market at EGP 120,000 to 300,000 a month, for one person, not a team. For a product that is your core business and that you develop daily, an in-house team pays off better over the long run.

How do I make sure the developer or company does not vanish after the first payment?

There is no absolute guarantee, but you can make a disappearance harmless: small payments tied to working deliveries, code pushed to a repository you own as it is written, every account in your name, and a written contract. Then the most you lose is one stage, and another team continues from where the first stopped.

Should I work with a company inside Saudi Arabia or outside it?

The decision follows the way of working more than the place. Require overlapping hours within your days and time zone, a clear contract, regular test builds and knowledge of the Saudi market’s requirements. If the vendor is in another country, review the contract and dispute resolution with your lawyer, and the transfer of personal data with your adviser.

How do I follow a team working remotely?

With a steady rhythm, not constant monitoring: a short weekly meeting, a working test build every week or two, a task board you can see, and one daily channel for questions. Make the build you try yourself the measure of progress, not the reports. And appoint one person on your side who owns the decisions.

Who owns the code in outsourced projects?

It should be yours, written into the contract with the point at which ownership passes. What matters more in practice is that the code is pushed to a repository you own from day one, and that the servers and accounts are in your company’s name. Ownership on paper with no real access does not help you when you need it.

Is my company’s data safe with an outside team?

It is safer if the team works on test data, access to the live environment goes to as few people as possible, a non-disclosure agreement is signed, and the contract states where hosting is and what happens to the data when the engagement ends. Your duties under the Personal Data Protection Law, especially when data is transferred outside the Kingdom, are for your adviser to confirm.

What is the difference between a dedicated team and staff augmentation?

With a dedicated team you get a complete team with a project manager from the vendor; you set priorities and the vendor runs execution. With staff augmentation you get individuals who join your team and work under your management and in your way. The first suits a company with no technical management; the second requires it.

How do I start outsourcing for the first time?

Start small: a paid piece with a clear result, such as a requirements study, the interface design or one module. Within weeks you will know whether the team keeps dates and asks the right questions. Open the code repository and the accounts in your name before the first line is written, and put the scope and payments in a contract.
