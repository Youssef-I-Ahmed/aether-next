Who is this for?

  • Small and mid-sized factories running production on Excel and shift sheets
  • Make-to-order factories whose specifications and sizes differ from customer to customer
  • Food and chemical producers that need batch and expiry traceability
  • Production workshops that have grown and now need planning and accurate costing
  • Factories whose accounting software knows nothing about the shop floor

What you get

Bills of materials and routings

Each product’s components with quantities and scrap rates, and its manufacturing steps, work centres and standard times, in documented versions.

Production orders

An order raised from a customer order or the stock plan reserves materials and tracks every stage through to receipt of finished goods.

Material requirements planning

From orders, balances and lead times the system proposes what to buy and when, before a line stops for a missing material.

Line scheduling and capacity

Orders loaded onto lines, machines and shifts, showing bottlenecks and realistic delivery dates.

Batch-tracked stock with barcodes

Raw, semi-finished and finished goods with batch numbers and expiry dates, and movements scanned by barcode from a phone.

Shop-floor production reporting

The supervisor records output, scrap, stoppages and their causes on a touch screen or phone at the line.

Actual product cost

Materials, labour, machine running and overheads charged to the order, with actual compared to standard and variances analysed.

Quality and traceability

Inspections at receipt, in process and before dispatch, with any product traceable back to its material batch and supplier, and forward again.

Machine maintenance

Preventive maintenance plans by hours or interval, maintenance orders and spare parts, and a breakdown history for each machine.

The question accounting software can’t answer: what did this unit cost me?

Most small and mid-sized factories price from an estimated cost set years ago. Since then raw material prices have moved, scrap has risen on one line, and run times have stretched on another. The result is products sold at a thinner margin than management believes, and others priced higher than they should be, losing orders.

The system costs from reality: the materials actually issued to the order, the labour and machine hours recorded, and the overheads allocated. It then compares that with standard and shows where the variance came from: material price, excess scrap or longer time. Pricing turns from a guess into a decision.

Off-the-shelf manufacturing ERP or a custom system?

Ready-made systems with a manufacturing module are a good choice when your production is standard: fixed products with fixed bills of materials. But cost them over three years, with annual per-user licences and implementation and customisation fees, not on the first month’s subscription.

Custom wins when your factory has its own logic: a product calculated from dimensions and profiles, pricing that depends on material weight and the day’s price, toll manufacturing, or by-products from the same process. Bending a ready-made system to these cases often costs more than building them directly. And with a custom system you own the code and the data, and growth isn’t tied to the number of licences.

There is a middle path too: if your accounting software is good we keep it, build the production, planning and costing modules, and connect them to it.

Connecting to accounting, e-invoicing and the shop floor

Every movement in the factory has an accounting effect: material issue, work in progress, receipt of finished goods, sale. The entries are posted automatically, so the stock value in the books matches what is actually in the warehouse. Sales invoices are issued in line with ZATCA’s e-invoicing requirements, integrated with the Fatoora platform for businesses in scope.

On the shop floor we connect scales, barcode scanners and label printers. Reading data from the machines themselves is possible where the machine or its controller exposes a data output, and we assess that machine by machine instead of making a blanket promise. Over WhatsApp, management gets the alerts that matter: a material below minimum, an order behind schedule, a machine stopped.

  • Automatic entries for issue, production and sale at cost
  • E-invoices from sales orders and deliveries
  • Scales, barcodes and batch label printers
  • WhatsApp alerts for shortages, delays and stoppages

Make to order or make to stock: the system follows your method

A make-to-order factory needs everything to start from the quotation: the customer’s specification becomes a bill of materials and an estimated cost, then a sales order, then a production order that carries the order number through every stage to delivery. The salesperson knows what stage their customer’s order is at without phoning the factory.

A make-to-stock factory needs the opposite: a demand forecast, a minimum level for each finished item, and a weekly production plan that fills stock with the fewest changeovers. Many factories combine both, and the system supports both on one database.

What to prepare before the first meeting

Accurate bills of materials are the foundation of everything: planning, issuing and costing. If they aren’t documented, documenting them is the first task in the project, and we help with it.

  • Bills of materials for the main products, even if they are in Excel or in the production manager’s head
  • Manufacturing stages, lines, machines and shifts
  • Your current work order, production report and quality forms
  • How pricing and costing are done today
  • Your accounting software, stock balances and the last stock count

Common mistakes and how to compare quotes

The first mistake is trying to schedule every machine to the minute from day one. Start with production orders, issue and receipt, and leave detailed scheduling for a later phase once the data has settled. The second is going live on stock balances that haven’t been counted, so the whole material plan is built on wrong numbers.

The third is ignoring the line supervisor. If the production entry screen has ten fields, it won’t be filled in. When comparing quotes, ask: are actual costing and variance analysis in scope? Is batch traceability included? Is there a licence per user? And who owns the code?

Frequently asked questions

How much does an ERP for a factory cost?

A manufacturing ERP with us starts from EGP 282,000. Production and costing modules and the number of lines set the final price, and we send a written quote within 24 hours.

How is a manufacturing ERP different from accounting and stock software?

Accounting and stock software knows you bought a raw material and sold a product. A manufacturing system knows what happens in between: the bill of materials, the production order, work in progress, scrap and machine hours. That is why it can plan materials and calculate actual product cost, which trading software can’t.

Does it support make-to-order and custom sizes?

Yes. We build a product configurator that turns the customer’s specification, such as size, colour and material, into a bill of materials, a cost and a price automatically, and then into a production order. This kind of specific logic is the strongest case for a custom system, because bending ready-made systems to it is hard and expensive.

Does it calculate actual product cost?

Yes. Each production order is charged with the materials actually issued, labour, machine running cost, and its share of overheads on the basis your cost accountant sets. It is then compared with standard cost, and the variance is shown with its causes, per order, product and period.

Does it track batches and expiry dates?

Yes. Every material receipt and production order has a batch number, so any finished product can be traced to its materials and their suppliers, and every product that used a given material batch can be found. Issuing can follow first-expiry-first-out where needed. This is essential for food and chemical producers.

Does it connect directly to the machines?

It depends on the machine. Where the machine or its controller exposes a data output, counters and stoppages can be read automatically, and we assess each machine before promising it. Otherwise the supervisor records output on a simple screen at the line, which is enough for most mid-sized factories.

We have accounting software. Do we replace it?

Not necessarily. If it serves accounts and allows integration, we build the production, planning and costing modules and post entries to it. We recommend replacement when stock in the current software doesn’t support batches or semi-finished goods, at which point integration becomes harder than building.

Is it compliant with e-invoicing?

Yes. Sales invoices are issued in the required format with a QR code, integrated with the Fatoora platform for businesses covered by the integration phase. The invoice is tied to the sales order and delivery note, so nothing is invoiced that wasn’t delivered and nothing is delivered that wasn’t invoiced.

How long does it take, and where do we start?

The first module goes live in 6 to 10 weeks and the complete system in 3 to 6 months. We usually start with stock, bills of materials and production orders, then planning and costing, then quality and maintenance. The date of each phase is written into the contract and we keep it.

Does the factory own the code?

Yes. The source code, database and hosting accounts are in your business’s name and the contract says so, with no licences per user. The system can be hosted on a server inside the factory or in the cloud. After launch you get fast technical support.
